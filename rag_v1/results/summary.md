Overall, the hierarchical RAPTOR retrieval system provides improvements in context efficiency and scalability, though the baseline text-only retriever achieved higher retrieval accuracy on this dataset. The baseline answered 17 out of 20 queries in the top-5 (Recall@5 ≈ 85%), compared to 15 out of 20 for RAPTOR (Recall@5 ≈ 75%). Rank quality followed a similar pattern: the baseline’s nDCG@5 was 0.69 vs. RAPTOR’s 0.60. Because most evaluation queries required only textual evidence, multimodal retrieval (text+image) performed comparably to text-only, with no significant recall gain.

Where RAPTOR excels is in context efficiency. Its cluster-guided search retrieved ~12% fewer words per query on average, often selecting a single representative passage rather than multiple redundant ones. This reduces input length for downstream models without substantially hurting answerability.

In terms of latency, RAPTOR’s two-stage retrieval was slower on small corpora (median 149 ms vs. 28 ms for the baseline). However, RAPTOR’s latency scaled more favorably as the corpus size grew from 23 to 1023 papers: baseline latency increased roughly linearly, whereas RAPTOR’s hierarchical coarse-to-fine search grew more slowly.

Scaling behavior. As the corpus grew, baseline recall improved because more relevant papers became available, though nDCG dipped slightly due to increased distractors. RAPTOR maintained relatively stable recall and ranking quality by narrowing the search space to relevant clusters. The multimodal baseline remained similar to text-only because the queries did not strongly rely on visual evidence; we expect multimodal retrieval to show clearer advantages on figure- or diagram-focused tasks.

When does RAPTOR help most? RAPTOR was most effective on broad or ambiguous queries where many papers partially matched the topic. In these cases, its cluster-level retrieval identified the correct subset of documents, improving recall where flat retrieval sometimes missed the target paper. For highly specific queries containing distinctive terms or names, the baseline already performed strongly and RAPTOR provided less benefit.

Limitations. RAPTOR’s effectiveness depends on the quality of hierarchical summaries (L2 and L1). Coarse or incomplete summaries can misdirect the search to an irrelevant cluster. We observed a few cases where this caused RAPTOR to miss papers that the baseline retrieved. Additionally, maintaining the hierarchical index introduces some complexity and preprocessing overhead, though retrieval remains efficient once built. Multimodal retrieval offered little benefit here due to the text-centric nature of the questions, but is expected to help more when visual grounding is required.
